# Frog Life Cycle, Maturity, and Survival
Date: 2026-11-08  
Scope: Summary of this conversation, including corrections and the sources consulted.

## Core ideas

A typical frog life cycle is:

**Egg → Tadpole → Metamorphosing young frog → Juvenile frog → Sexually mature adult → Eggs**

- **Egg:** An embryo develops inside an egg, usually protected by jelly. Many species lay eggs in water.
- **Tadpole:** The aquatic larva has a tail and initially relies largely on gills. Many eat algae or other plant material, but diets vary.
- **Metamorphosis:** Hind legs generally appear before front legs; lungs and other organs develop or change, and the tail is absorbed. Its materials are recycled by the body.
- **Juvenile:** The young frog resembles an adult but is not yet sexually mature.
- **Adult:** A sexually mature frog can reproduce.

Timing varies with species and environmental conditions. Some frogs develop directly inside terrestrial eggs and hatch as small frogs rather than free-swimming tadpoles.

## Froglet versus adult: the key distinction

**Completing metamorphosis and reaching sexual maturity are separate milestones.** Losing the tail does not make a frog reproductively mature.

“Froglet” is an informal or study-specific term for a young frog around or after metamorphosis. It does not universally mean an animal that still has a tail. This corrects the initial simplified explanation in the conversation.

Researchers define developmental groups for the species being studied. A captive Pickersgill’s reed-frog study used approximately 9 weeks–6 months for froglets, 6–18 months for subadults, and about 18 months onward for sexually mature adults. Those ages are specific to that study, not universal frog thresholds. [Source 1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10078702/)

Field classifications can combine body size, reproductive features such as male nuptial pads, and breeding observations. Size is useful but does not by itself establish maturity. [Source 2](https://esajournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/ecs2.3381)

## Can tadpoles reproduce?

**No: frog tadpoles must complete metamorphosis and later reach sexual maturity before reproducing.** A tailless juvenile may still be unable to produce mature eggs or sperm.

The important sequence is development into the frog body form, followed by reproductive maturation; these need not happen at the same time.

## Mortality and survival: interpreting the numbers

There is no universal mortality percentage for each frog stage. Every estimate needs a species, setting, starting stage, endpoint, and time interval.

Examples discussed:

- **Captive eggs:** In the reed-frog study, mean hatching success was about 66%. The remaining eggs were described as infertile, so the roughly 34% that did not hatch should not all be counted as embryo deaths.
- **Captive tadpoles:** That study reported approximately 17% tadpole mortality. Captive results should not be treated as wild-pond averages. [Source 1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10078702/)
- **Wild early-life survival:** A model of two foothill yellow-legged frog populations estimated about 0.2% survival from egg to the two-year-old subadult stage—roughly 2 survivors per 1,000 eggs. This combines several stages and cannot isolate froglet mortality.
- **Wild adult survival:** At Fox Creek, estimated mean annual adult survival was approximately 46%. This was apparent survival: permanent emigration could not be separated from death. Consequently, the complementary 54% is not a clean mortality estimate. [Source 2](https://esajournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/ecs2.3381)

These examples are from different settings and should not be combined into one numerical life cycle. The conversation did not establish a separate, generally applicable wild froglet mortality rate.

### How tadpoles detect danger

Tadpoles use several sources of sensory information. Their responses vary with species, developmental stage, predator, and context.

- **Chemical sensing:** Chemicals dissolved in water can indicate predation risk. Common frog and common toad tadpoles reduced swimming after exposure to chemical cues from dragonfly larvae that had eaten tadpoles of the same species. This supports detection of active predation; it does not mean every tadpole species releases a deliberate alarm signal. [Chemical-cue experiment](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22972229/)
- **Vision:** Some tadpoles use visual predator information together with chemical cues. In an experiment with the poison frog *Allobates femoralis*, tadpoles avoided dragonfly larvae when both cues were present, but not when either was presented alone. This finding is species- and experiment-specific. [Combined-cue experiment](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8729909/)
- **Water-movement sensing:** The lateral line contains sensory organs with hair cells that respond to water movement. In African clawed frog (*Xenopus laevis*) tadpoles, nearby suction triggered turning and swimming away. Disabling the lateral-line sensors eliminated the response, supporting their role in escape behavior relevant to suction-feeding predators. [Lateral-line experiment](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8794055/)
- **Touch:** Stimulation of the skin can initiate swimming even in very young tadpoles. Touch and lateral-line sensing are related mechanical senses but detect different kinds of stimulation. [Sensory-development research](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8794055/)

Avoidance can involve reducing movement, moving away from a dangerous location, or rapidly escaping an immediate disturbance. There is no single response shared by all tadpoles.

### How senses change during metamorphosis

Metamorphosis reorganizes sensory organs and their brain connections. For species that become terrestrial, this supports the transition from sensing underwater to sensing in air. Aquatic adult frogs provide important exceptions.

- **Lateral line:** Frogs that become terrestrial generally lose this water-movement system. Some aquatic species, including African clawed frogs, retain it as adults. [Lateral-line research](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8794055/)
- **Vision:** In *Xenopus*, the eyes shift upward and forward during and after metamorphosis, increasing overlap between the two eyes' visual fields. Visual pathways develop alongside this change to support binocular processing. This example should not be treated as an identical pattern in every frog species. [Eye-position and visual-system study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3723067/)
- **Smell:** Nasal tissues and odor-detecting neurons undergo extensive remodeling. In *Xenopus*, the larval main olfactory region becomes an adult “air nose,” while a new region develops as a “water nose.” Many larval sensory neurons are replaced. [Olfactory-remodeling study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26294036/)
- **Hearing:** Tadpoles can detect sound before becoming frogs, while the ears and auditory pathways continue to develop. Bullfrog research found a temporary reduction in auditory responsiveness during metamorphosis. In some toads, middle-ear maturation continues for months afterward. These findings concern particular species and do not imply that all metamorphosing frogs become completely deaf. [Bullfrog auditory-development study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC25131/), [toad middle-ear study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27520654/)

Sensory changes occur on different schedules; a newly transformed frog may still have developing sensory abilities.

### Can memories survive metamorphosis?

**Evidence supports retention of some early learned preferences, but the exact neural mechanism remains unresolved.**

In a study of common frogs and wood frogs, embryos experienced particular odors before hatching. Tadpoles subsequently preferred the familiar odor, and this preference persisted after metamorphosis in individuals that had shown the preference as tadpoles. This was early embryonic learning expressed across later stages, rather than a demonstration that every type of memory acquired during tadpole life survives. [Hepper and Waldman: Embryonic olfactory learning in frogs](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1598418/)

### Why retention is biologically possible—and what remains uncertain

1. **Metamorphosis does not replace the entire nervous system.** Some neurons and connections persist into juvenile frogs. Evidence from movement pathways establishes neural continuity, but does not identify a memory-storage circuit. [Neural-continuity study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6564260/)
2. **Sensory remodeling can preserve function.** In *Xenopus*, responses to waterborne odors remained functional despite substantial olfactory reorganization. This supports continuity of sensory processing, not direct proof that a learned memory survived. [Olfactory-circuit experiment](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8595194/)
3. **A proposed explanation is persistence of learning-related changes in surviving circuits.** Enough connections or lasting changes within surviving neurons might remain to influence later behavior. The conversation did not identify research tracing a particular frog memory through metamorphosis at the cellular level; this explanation must remain labeled as a hypothesis.

In these experiments, “memory” means that earlier experience affects later behavior. The findings do not establish conscious recollection of tadpole life.

### Verification notes and common pitfalls

The sources linked above were consulted during the conversation. This update summarizes that discussion; it is not a comprehensive literature review.

- Findings from one species or developmental stage should not be generalized to all frogs.
- Detecting predator chemicals is different from deliberately secreting an alarm signal.
- Retaining an odor response is different from retaining a learned odor preference.
- Neuron survival in a movement pathway does not prove survival of a memory circuit.
- Behavioral evidence for retention does not establish the precise storage mechanism or human-like autobiographical recall.
